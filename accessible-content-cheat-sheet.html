Making Accessible Content Cheat Sheet

Accessible practices ensure screen readers, speech-to-text software, keyboard-only navigation, magnification tools, captions, and other assistive technologies can access the same information as everyone else. It is also a legal requirement for public bodies. The best way to find out if your content is accessible is to run the in-built accessibility checker in MS Word, PowerPoint or Excel. If information cannot be accessed, understood, navigated or interacted with by all users, it is not fully accessible.

1. Structure and Navigation

Structure and navigation: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Structure Use built-in Heading styles and a logical hierarchy. Use PowerPoint's inbuilt layouts as they are already formatted for assistive technologies. Manually formatting text to look like headings. Screen reader users cannot navigate efficiently between sections and may have to read the entire document from start to finish.
Titles Give every document and slide a unique, meaningful title. Blank or repeated titles. Screen reader users may not know where they are in the document or presentation.
Reading Order Check the reading order in PowerPoint. Assuming visual order matches screen reader order. Content may be read in a confusing sequence.
Lists Use proper bulleted and numbered lists. Creating lists manually using symbols, tabs or spacing. Assistive technologies may not recognise the content as a list.
Hyperlinks Use meaningful link text that describes the destination. "Click here", "More", or pasting long URLs. Users navigating by links alone cannot determine where the link goes.
File Names Use meaningful file names. Vague names such as "Document1" or "Final_Final_v3". Users may struggle to identify the correct file.
Document Titles Add a meaningful title in document properties. Leaving the default title. Screen reader users may be unable to distinguish between documents.

2. Text and Readability

Text and readability: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Fonts Use clear sans-serif fonts and readable font sizes. Decorative, handwritten or overly stylised fonts. Text may be difficult to read for people with dyslexia, low vision or cognitive impairments.
Text Alignment Keep text left-aligned. Fully justified text. Uneven spacing can make reading more difficult.
Plain Language Use clear language and explain jargon, acronyms and technical terms. Complex language or unexplained terminology. Users may understand the words but not the meaning.
Acronyms Define acronyms the first time they are used. Assuming everyone knows specialist abbreviations. Users may not understand the information provided.
Reading Ease Use adequate spacing between lines and paragraphs. Dense, crowded text. Content becomes more difficult to track and process.

3. Visual Design

Visual design: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Colour Contrast Use sufficient contrast between text and background. Low-contrast colour combinations. Content may be unreadable for some users.
Colour Usage Use labels, patterns or text as well as colour. Using colour alone to convey meaning. Users with colour vision deficiencies may miss important information.
Images Add meaningful alt text to informative images. Leaving images without alt text. Screen readers cannot communicate the information contained in the image.
Decorative Images Mark decorative images as decorative. Adding unnecessary alt text to decorative images. Screen reader users may have to listen to irrelevant content.
Charts and Graphs Provide labels and explain key findings in text. Presenting visual information without explanation. Users who cannot see the chart may miss the key message.

4. Tables and Data

Tables and data: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Tables Use simple tables with clearly identified header rows. Complex tables, merged cells, blank cells, or tables used for layout. Screen readers may read information incorrectly or users may struggle to understand relationships between data.
Searchability Ensure text can be selected and searched. Image-based content that cannot be searched. Users cannot easily locate information or use study-support tools effectively.

5. Multimedia

Multimedia: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Video Provide captions. Uncaptioned videos. Deaf and hard-of-hearing users may not be able to access the content.
Audio Provide transcripts. Audio recordings without transcripts. Users who cannot hear the recording cannot access the information.
Multiple Formats Provide information in more than one format where possible. Providing content in only one format. Some learners may be unable to access information using their preferred method.

6. PDFs and Scanned Documents

PDFs and scanned documents: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
PDFs Create PDFs from accessible Word or PowerPoint files and check accessibility before sharing. Printing or scanning directly to PDF without accessibility checks. Important accessibility features such as headings and reading order may be lost.
Scanned Documents Use OCR (Optical Character Recognition) to convert scans into accessible text. Uploading image-only scans. Screen readers cannot access the text content.
Text as Images Use editable text whenever possible. If text must appear in an image, provide alternative text. Sharing screenshots, posters or documents containing text as images. Users cannot search, resize, translate, copy or have the content read aloud.

7. Neurodiversity and Cognitive Accessibility

Neurodiversity and cognitive accessibility: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Cognitive Load Break content into short sections with clear headings and white space. Large blocks of dense text. Users may struggle to locate, process and remember information.
Consistency Use consistent layouts, colours, terminology and navigation. Frequently changing formats and layouts. Users spend effort understanding the format instead of the content.
Sensory Overload Keep slides uncluttered and focused on key messages. Busy slides with excessive text, images and colours. Content can become overwhelming and difficult to process.
Animation and Movement Use animation only when it adds value. Excessive, flashing or auto-playing content. Can distract users and increase cognitive load.
Pace of Information Allow sufficient time for users to read and process content. Auto-advancing slides or rapidly changing information. Users may not have enough time to read, understand or take notes.

8. Final Checks Before Sharing

Final checks before sharing: dos, avoids, and consequences
Area Do Avoid What happens if you don't?
Accessibility Checker Run the Accessibility Checker before sharing. Assuming content is accessible without testing. Accessibility barriers may remain unnoticed until experienced by users.
Keyboard Accessibility Ensure content can be navigated using a keyboard where relevant. Requiring a mouse for essential interactions. Users with motor impairments or screen reader users may be unable to access all content.

Accessible Content Cheat Sheet for creating documents, slides and other digital content. Generative AI tool Microsoft Copilot and AI-powered editing in Visual Studio Code was used to aid in the creation of this resource.

A note on ARIA and HTML: when making web content, documents, or slides accessible, always use native HTML elements and structures first (headings, lists, tables, buttons, links, form labels, etc.), since they come with built-in semantics and keyboard support that assistive technologies already understand. Only add ARIA roles, states and properties to fill gaps that native HTML cannot express, such as custom interactive widgets or live regions. Unnecessary or incorrect ARIA can do more harm than no ARIA at all, so it should be used to enhance native semantics, and not replace them.
